Can Airplanes Go Faster?

Published: March 05, 2026

A week ago, when I was taking a long flight—like a 15-hour flight back to Taiwan—I start wondering about something. Why does it still take this long?

Over the past 10 years, airplane travel time between countries feels almost identical. Taipei to the United States still takes roughly the same amount of time. Europe to Asia also feels the same.

On land, transportation clearly became faster. Trains evolved into high-speed rail. If you are willing to pay more, you can simply travel faster.

But airplanes do not seem to follow this pattern.


Airplanes can actually go faster

Interestingly, airplanes can go much faster. Supersonic passenger aircraft once existed.

The Concorde could fly at about twice the speed of sound and cross the Atlantic in about three and a half hours.

So the real question is not whether airplanes can go faster.

The question is: why don't we fly that fast anymore?


The real constraint: physics and economics

When airplanes fly faster, air resistance increases dramatically. This means the energy required to maintain speed grows very quickly.

A slightly faster flight can require much more fuel. For airlines, this becomes extremely expensive.

Because of this, the aviation industry settled on a speed that is not the fastest possible, but the most efficient balance between time, fuel, and cost.


What actually improved in the last decade

Modern airplanes usually cruise around Mach 0.8 to 0.85, and this number has barely changed for decades.

In the past 10 years airplanes did not really become faster. Instead, they became more efficient, quieter, and cheaper to operate.


Reflection

I find this interesting.

Technological progress is not always about pushing the maximum possible performance. Sometimes the optimal solution is simply the most efficient balance between physics, economics, and practicality.

Maybe one day supersonic passenger travel will return. But until then, when we sit on a long flight across the ocean, we are basically traveling at the same speed people used decades ago.

Never mind. I'll just buy a jet.
